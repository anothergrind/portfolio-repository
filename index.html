Geomagnetic Storm Predictor

Forecasting Space Weather · 3-Hour Prediction

Geomagnetic storms can disrupt satellites, GPS, aviation systems, and power infrastructure. This project builds three machine learning classifiers to predict whether a geomagnetic storm will occur in the next 3 hours from OMNI solar-wind parameters, solar-flare activity, and CME data. We trained and deployed XGBoost, LSTM, and TCN models on 30 years of observations, evaluated with chronological splits to avoid time leakage, and shipped them behind an interactive Streamlit dashboard for side-by-side comparison and real-time forecasting.

Overview

🌌

The Challenge: Geomagnetic storms are rare but high-impact events. A naive model can achieve great accuracy scores while catching almost nothing. We needed to predict whether the Ap index (a measure of geomagnetic disturbance) will reach storm thresholds in the next 3 hours — balancing sensitivity to true storms against the cost of false alarms.

Our Solution: We built a time-binned dataset from OMNI solar-wind parameters, NASA DONKI CME events, solar-flare counts, and geomagnetic indices, spanning 1995–2024. We trained three different model architectures — XGBoost for interpretability, LSTM for sequential dependencies, and TCN for causal temporal convolution — all with chronological train/test splits to prevent time leakage. Each model uses different strategies to handle severe class imbalance: oversampling, pos-weighted loss, and focal loss. The final models are deployed behind an interactive Streamlit dashboard.

The modeling dataset combines multiple public space-weather sources into 3-hour time bins covering 1995–2024. Each row represents the information available during one 3-hour interval and is used to determine whether a geomagnetic storm occurs three hours later.

Data Sources

The project combines publicly available space-weather observations and event data from several sources. The source datasets were processed and aggregated into 3-hour intervals for use in model training and evaluation.

  • NASA/GSFC OMNIWeb — near-Earth solar-wind plasma and interplanetary magnetic-field measurements, including variables such as solar-wind speed, proton density, and magnetic-field components.
  • NASA DONKI — space-weather event information used to derive features related to coronal mass ejections (CMEs) and other solar events.
  • Solar Flare Data — solar-flare event information used to construct flare-related features.
  • Geomagnetic Index Data — geomagnetic activity indices, including the 3-hourly ap index, used in constructing the storm target and related features.

The resulting dataset contains 87,649 three-hour observations spanning 1995–2024. Features from these sources were combined and time-aligned so that information available at a given time could be used to predict geomagnetic storm conditions three hours later.

Models

All three classifiers predict whether the Ap index will reach 50 (roughly Kp 5 storm conditions) in the next 3 hours. Each is trained on 2010–2021 and evaluated on the same held-out 2022–2024 test period. The models trade off different strengths: XGBoost offers interpretability and high recall; LSTM achieves the highest precision and PR-AUC; TCN delivers the best overall F1 balance.

01 Storm Predictor — XGBoost

A gradient-boosted tree classifier that forecasts storm risk 3 hours ahead from 45 engineered features of the current 3-hour bin — solar wind, magnetic field, flare counts, CME activity, and 27-day recurrence — with no current Ap features. Highest recall of the three, and the most interpretable baseline.

DatasetSingle 3-hour bins, 2010–2024 (OMNI + DONKI + flares)
ModelXGBoost + RandomOverSampler, threshold 0.90
ResultPR-AUC 0.575 · Recall 0.684 · F1 0.485

02 Storm Predictor — LSTM

A 2×128 recurrent sequence model trained with pos-weighted BCE that reads a rolling 48-hour window of 8 solar-wind and Ap channels instead of a single bin. Best precision and PR-AUC of the three — when it calls a storm, it's usually right.

Dataset48-hour sequences (16 bins × 8 channels), 2010–2024
ModelLSTM 2×128, pos-weighted BCE, threshold 0.795
ResultPR-AUC 0.610 · Precision 0.729 · F1 0.555

03 Storm Predictor — TCN

A causal dilated convolutional network trained with focal loss on a rolling 48-hour solar-wind history, without ever seeing the current Ap index. Architecture and imbalance strategy were both chosen on a chronological validation split, then refit once on the full training window. Best overall F1 — the most balanced of the three.

Dataset48-hour solar-wind sequences, no current Ap, 2010–2024
ModelTCN 4×64, focal loss (α .75, γ 2), threshold 0.60

Model Evaluation

Because geomagnetic storms are rare events, accuracy alone would not adequately describe model performance. We therefore compared the models using PR-AUC, precision, recall, F1, and ROC-AUC on the same held-out 2022–2024 test period.

XGBoost PR-AUC 0.575 · Precision 0.375 · Recall 0.684 · F1 0.485
LSTM PR-AUC 0.610 · Precision 0.729 · Recall 0.448 · F1 0.555
TCN PR-AUC 0.602 · Precision 0.611 · Recall 0.552 · F1 0.580
XGBoost PR-AUC 0.575 Recall · 0.684
LSTM PR-AUC 0.610 Precision · 0.729
TCN F1 0.580 Best overall balance

Interpretation: XGBoost catches the largest proportion of actual storms, but produces more false alarms. LSTM has the strongest precision and PR-AUC, meaning its positive predictions are more reliable. TCN achieves the highest F1 score, providing the best balance between precision and recall at its selected threshold.

Operating thresholds were tuned rather than fixed at 0.50 because the positive storm class is highly imbalanced.

Performance comparison

The models demonstrate different strengths because they use different representations of recent space-weather conditions. XGBoost operates on a single 3-hour feature vector, while the LSTM and TCN process approximately 48 hours of historical observations.

Impact & Bias

Potential Impact

Earlier warning of geomagnetic storms could help satellite operators, aviation systems, communications networks, and power infrastructure prepare for potentially disruptive space-weather events. A model that improves storm detection could provide an additional tool for monitoring and decision-making. [2,4,6,7]

However, false alarms and missed storms both have consequences. Excessive false positives could lead to unnecessary operational responses, while false negatives could cause users to underestimate an approaching storm. This system should therefore be treated as a decision-support tool rather than a replacement for expert space-weather forecasting.

Bias & Responsible AI

Machine-learning models can reflect biases in the data used to train them. Geomagnetic storms are relatively rare, so severe class imbalance can cause a model to favor the majority non-storm class. We addressed this through oversampling, weighted losses, focal loss, and threshold tuning rather than relying on accuracy alone.

Model performance may also vary across different types or intensities of space-weather events. Evaluating precision, recall, PR-AUC, and confusion matrices helps reveal these trade-offs, while the chronological test split provides a more realistic estimate of performance on future observations.
